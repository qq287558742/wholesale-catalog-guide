Reading a Wholesale Catalog

Most first-time buyers read a wholesale catalog the way they read a retail listing: price first. The fields that actually decide whether a SKU survives two seasons are different. Notes from DPJ Wholesale, a B2B catalog covering 65,000 consumer SKUs.

The five fields worth reading

Case pack
How many units arrive per order line. A cheap unit cost with a case pack of 48 is not a cheap buy; it is a commitment.
Unit dimensions and weight
Decides freight, shelf space and whether the item ships alone or as part of a mixed pallet. Catalogs that omit this force the buyer to guess, and guessing shows up as dead stock.
Reorder rate
The share of accounts that bought the same SKU a second time. This is the only field that measures a decision someone already repeated with their own money.
Return rate
A cost of goods, not a customer-service metric. A category returning 20% of units needs a materially different margin than one returning 4%.
Size consistency
Whether the second unit fits the first. If it does not, the customer does not come back, and no amount of pricing fixes that.

Why unit cost is the weakest field

Unit cost is the easiest number to compare and the least predictive. Two SKUs at the same unit cost can differ by a factor of three in landed cost once freight, breakage and the capital tied up in a case pack are counted. Where a catalog shows unit cost alone, treat it as a starting point rather than a decision.

A short checklist before committing

Before placing a first order on a new line: confirm the case pack, confirm the unit dimensions, confirm the recommended retail band, and check whether the category has a replacement part or consumable attached to it. A line with a consumable attached will reorder even when the main unit does not.

What to stop stocking

Stop when the reorder rate falls below the cost of the shelf space the SKU occupies — not when it stops selling entirely. A slow mover that never reorders is consuming capacity a proven repeat line would use better.
